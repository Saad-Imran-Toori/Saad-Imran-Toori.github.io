DecisAI — an AI bid-response system for RFPs and tenders

CUST Hackathon 2026 · team of three · my part: the retrieval layer

The problem

Proposal teams get RFPs and tenders running to hundreds of pages. Someone has to read every line, pull out the requirements, and work out whether the company can actually deliver — usually against a deadline. It is slow, repetitive, and easy to miss something that matters.

What I did

A company first uploads its own bid history and capability records. I chunked and embedded those with Sentence Transformers and stored them in ChromaDB, so that when a new tender arrives, each extracted requirement can be matched against what the company has actually done before. Claude Haiku runs it online; Ollama phi3:mini handles the offline case.

The decision that shaped the system: it drafts a response only where it finds supporting evidence. Where there is none, it marks the requirement “no match found” instead of writing something that sounds right.

The same rule holds at the end of the pipeline. The system is willing to recommend against bidding at all, and says why.

What was hard

Retrieval was the hard part, not the model. It was my first time building this, and large documents kept breaking it — anything past about 1,500 pages was slow and sometimes came back with nothing usable. It was a hackathon, so I made a call: get it reliable at working sizes and stop there, rather than chase scale the judges would never see.

What came of it

91

94%
extraction confidence

Each requirement was checked against the company’s past evidence, and anything unsupported was marked rather than hidden. We did not place, but we finished with participation badges and certificates, and the judges gave good feedback on the approach.

What I would do differently: spend real time on retrieval performance for very large documents. Past 1,500 pages it is still slow, and that is the first thing I would fix.

Prioritising 16,726 pages for review

Machine Learning internship at FlyRank AI · in progress

The problem

FlyRank has around 16,726 content pages worth checking, and an editor can realistically review about 50 a week. That is a 335-week backlog. So the useful question is not “is this page underperforming?” — thousands are. It is “which page should someone open first?”

What I did

I chose the click-through opportunity lane: find pages that get shown in search but under-capture clicks compared with similar pages. My first idea was simple. Every page sits in a search-position tier, so score each page by how far its click rate falls below the typical rate for its tier, then rank by that gap.

Before building anything on top of that, I tested whether the assumption held. It did not. The tier rule turned out to be only 5.6% better than ignoring position entirely and predicting one flat number for every page. Worse, its top 100 pages all received the exact same score — one distinct value across the entire top of the queue. So the rule could not order the one part of the list that actually gets used.

The same standard, applied to myself

Later in the internship I ran a deliberate leakage experiment on the real warehouse: I added one column derived from the answer itself and watched a model’s score jump from an honest 0.037 to 0.874 — looking almost perfect while actually cheating. Then I removed it and kept the honest number.

What came of it

The write-up had to be redone. I had already explained why the tier rule was a reasonable baseline, and the data said otherwise, so that section was rewritten to report the real numbers instead. I wanted the submission to be correct rather than tidy.

The rule became a frozen baseline rather than the product. A model trained on it — validated with a client-level holdout so it is never tested on a client it has seen — now beats that rule in every fold. About half of that gain comes from click history, which I report alongside the headline rather than under it.

What I would do differently: test the assumption before writing the explanation of it. I wrote the reasoning for the tier rule first and checked it second, which is why the section needed rewriting.
